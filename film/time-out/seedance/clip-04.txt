SCENE CONTEXT & STYLE

What happens: A few minutes later, inside the beige house. The living room is a mess of toys. GLENN KOWALSKI, Brayden's dad, an ex-army guy, sits in the middle of the saggy couch holding a coffee mug, staring at nothing with a big frozen grin. He hasn't blinked in a while. A toy car rolls across the rug. Then BRAYDEN marches in with a red crayon in his mouth, climbs up onto the coffee table so he's level with his dad, and spits the crayon into Glenn's coffee. Glenn doesn't even flinch. Brayden orders his dad to take a nap. Glenn slowly turns to look at him and gets emotional, because Brayden has never once wanted him to take a nap. Brayden flashes a way-too-big fake smile, his eyes darting everywhere, the worst liar alive, and claims he just needs it because he's growing. The joke of the scene: anyone can see Brayden is up to something, except his dad, who thinks he's an angel.

Continuity: Brayden has just come downstairs from the upstairs window in the previous scene. He is in his regular look, with no face paint and no helmet. Glenn has no ear protectors and no sleep mask yet; those come in the next clip.

Tone and pace: a dry, deadpan comedy. Glenn is slow, flat and frozen; Brayden is quick, bossy and stompy. The contrast between them is the joke. Leave a real pause after every line before anyone reacts or answers. Nobody rushes and nobody talks over anyone.

Look: stylized 3D animated feature film, like a modern big-studio CG movie. Exaggerated cartoon character designs, big expressive faces, smooth sculpted forms, soft warm subsurface shading, detailed fabric, hair and beard textures, clean appealing shapes, warm saturated colors, soft global illumination. The rendering style matches the reference images exactly and stays identical from the first frame to the last.

Color palette: warm beige walls, a tan rug, the brown corduroy couch, bright primary-colored toys, and warm bars of afternoon sunlight. The strongest color accent in this clip is the red crayon. No color grading changes between shots.

Camera language for this film: eye level or close to it, real lenses between 24mm and 85mm, natural perspective with straight vertical lines. Camera moves are slow and smooth. No fisheye, no Dutch angles, no drone shots, no whip pans, no zooms, no shaky handheld.

Acting and movement: grounded and natural. The characters are designed like cartoons, but they move like real actors. Small, economical movements at real human speed, subtle facial expressions. Glenn barely moves at all: his stillness is the comedy. Brayden moves with short, quick, stomping steps and firm, bossy gestures, but nothing rubbery. No bouncing, no big arm-waving, no snappy cartoon poses. Every movement in this prompt is described exactly, including which hand or foot does it, so do exactly that movement and nothing extra.

Format: 20 seconds, 16:9, five shots joined by four hard cuts. Natural real-time motion at 24 fps. No slow motion, no speed ramps, no freeze frames.

Lighting: a sunny Saturday at about 2 PM, the same day as the rest of the film. Warm sunlight comes in through half-closed blinds on the big front window and lays soft, striped bars of light across the rug, the coffee table, the couch, Glenn and the back wall. When the camera looks at the couch, that window is behind the camera. When the camera looks toward the front of the room, the window blinds glow bright behind Brayden and his face is lit by soft, warm light bouncing off the room. The lamp beside the couch is off. The light is fixed to the world, not to the characters: when they move, the stripes of light travel across them. No flicker, no color shift, no change in time of day.

Physics: real weight, gravity and timing. The couch cushions sag under Glenn. The coffee table is sturdy: it creaks and shifts very slightly when Brayden climbs on it and stomps, but it never tips. The toy car rolls on the rug, slows down and stops against a building block. The crayon flies out of Brayden's mouth on a short arc and drops into the coffee with a small plop and a few tiny splashes on the rim of the mug. The mug doesn't move in Glenn's hand. Glenn's eyes get glossy and tears well up along his lower lids, but they never spill down his cheeks. Faces and bodies keep their shape: no squash and stretch.

Sound: diegetic sound only. No music, no score, no stingers. Clean dialogue with accurate lip sync, plus only the sounds the room would really make: a quiet house room tone, a wall clock ticking somewhere out of frame, a toy somewhere chirping its low-battery beep once, the toy car's plastic wheels on the rug and a soft clack when it stops, quick footsteps thumping on the carpet, the coffee table creaking, a wet spit sound, a plop in the coffee, one stomp on the table with a rattle of the things on it, a small sniff from Glenn.

12 PRINCIPLES OF ANIMATION (GROUNDED VERSION FOR THIS FILM)

1. Squash and stretch: almost none. Only natural movement of real faces, like cheeks shifting as someone talks. Faces never stretch, bulge or go rubbery. Brayden's fake smile is wide because that's his design, not because his face stretches.
2. Anticipation: small and natural, never a cartoon wind-up. Brayden puts his hands on the table before his knee goes up. He pulls his head back an inch before he spits. He takes a quick breath before his order. Glenn's eyes move toward Brayden a moment before his head turns.
3. Staging: one clear action at a time. The toy car in Shot 1, the climb and the spit in Shot 2, Brayden's order in Shot 3, Glenn's face in Shot 4 and the fake smile in Shot 5 are each the only thing happening in their shot.
4. Straight ahead and pose to pose: continuous, natural motion with no snapping into poses. During pauses, people simply hold still, the way real people do.
5. Follow-through and overlapping action: subtle. Glenn's clothes and the couch cushions settle as Brayden climbs onto the table next to him. The things on the coffee table rattle once after the stomp. A ripple spreads across the coffee after the crayon lands.
6. Slow in and slow out: every move starts and stops gently at human speed. Glenn's head turn is the slowest move in the clip, and the push-ins start and stop gently.
7. Arcs: the crayon flies on a short, natural arc, Glenn's head turns on a smooth arc, and Brayden's climb follows a natural arc up onto the table.
8. Secondary action: quiet background life that never pulls focus. The toy car rolls to a stop, dust drifts in the bars of sunlight, and the clock keeps ticking.
9. Timing: Glenn is slow and late, Brayden is quick and snappy. Leave the pauses between lines. Glenn's beat before his second line is long enough to feel.
10. Exaggeration: minimal. The comedy comes from contrast and timing: Glenn's total stillness against Brayden's bossiness, Glenn's wet eyes, Brayden's obviously fake innocence and darting eyes.
11. Solid drawing: consistent anatomy, weight and volume in every frame. Hands have five fingers and hold objects firmly. Nobody flattens, warps or melts.
12. Appeal: appealing designs and believable people. Glenn is a sweet, broken dad, and Brayden is a funny, charismatic tyrant. It's a comedy.
