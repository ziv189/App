SCENE CONTEXT & STYLE

What happens: The basement of the beige house, right after Brayden whispered "Company" upstairs. This is a gear-up montage in the style of an 80s action movie, played completely straight by BRAYDEN: six quick shots, each showing one single action. He pulls his dad's olive-green army footlocker out from under the basement stairs. He flips the latches and opens the lid, and a warm golden glow from inside lights up his face. He sits down with an old army field manual and studies it seriously, holding it upside down. He draws two stripes of black face paint under each eye. He drops his dad's oversized army helmet onto his head; it slides down over his face, and he shoves it back up. Finally he stands at the top of the basement stairs, backlit in the open doorway, cracks his neck, and delivers his action-hero line. We never see inside the footlocker, only its glow.

Continuity: Glenn is asleep on the couch upstairs and is never seen in this clip. Brayden starts in his regular look (<<<image_1>>>) and ends in his mission look (<<<image_2>>>): the face paint goes on in Shot 4 and the helmet in Shot 5, and both stay on from then on.

Tone and pace: a parody of an 80s action-movie gear-up montage, performed with total seriousness. The cutting is quick and snappy, one action per shot, but the acting inside each shot stays grounded and at real speed. The final shot is slower and heroic.

Look: stylized 3D animated feature film, like a modern big-studio CG movie. Exaggerated cartoon character designs, big expressive faces, smooth sculpted forms, soft warm subsurface shading, detailed fabric, metal, wood and concrete textures, warm saturated colors, soft global illumination. The rendering matches the reference images exactly and stays identical from the first frame to the last. Palette: grey concrete, off-white cinder block, raw wood, warm yellow bulb light, the olive-green footlocker and helmet, and the warm golden glow as the accent.

Camera language: real lenses between 35mm and 85mm, straight vertical lines, slow and smooth moves. No fisheye, no Dutch angles, no whip pans, no zooms, no shaky handheld. Shots 1 to 5 are at Brayden's eye level or close to it. Shot 6 is a low-angle hero shot, the only low angle in the clip.

Acting and movement: grounded and natural. Brayden is designed like a cartoon but moves like a real actor, at real human speed, with subtle facial expressions. He takes every step of this seriously, like a soldier gearing up for a mission: deliberate, precise, focused. No bouncing, no rubbery faces, no big gestures. Every movement is described exactly, including which hand does it: do exactly that and nothing extra.

Format: 20 seconds, 16:9, six shots joined by five hard cuts. Real-time motion at 24 fps. No slow motion, no speed ramps, no freeze frames.

Lighting: the dim, unfinished basement in the middle of a sunny afternoon. The main light is one bare bulb hanging from the middle of the ceiling, giving a warm yellow pool of light with hard shadows around it. A dusty shaft of daylight slants in through the small, high window. The door at the top of the stairs stands open, and the warm light of the living room behind it glows bright, which backlights Brayden in Shot 6. In Shots 2 and 3, a soft, warm golden glow rises from inside the open footlocker; it is the only extra light, and it only exists while the lid is open. Dust floats in the air. The light is fixed to the world, not to Brayden: when he moves, light and shadow slide across him. No flicker, no color shift.

Physics: real weight, gravity and timing. The footlocker is heavy: Brayden has to lean back with his whole weight, and it moves in short tugs, scraping on the concrete and kicking up small puffs of dust. The latches snap open. The lid swings up on its hinges and stays standing open. The manual's soft pages flop over when turned. The face paint is a thick, matte black cream that leaves clean stripes. The helmet is heavy steel and far too big: it drops onto his head, slides down over his whole face to his chin, and when he pushes it up it wobbles once and settles tilted, sitting low on his forehead and resting on top of his ears, which stick out underneath the rim. Faces and bodies keep their shape: no squash and stretch.

Sound: diegetic sound only. No music: the montage music is added later in editing. Clean dialogue with accurate lip sync, plus only these sounds, in order: the footlocker scraping on concrete and Brayden's short effort breaths; two latch clacks and a lid creak; soft page turns; the small pop of a tin lid and a soft smear; a heavy metal clunk of the helmet and a muffled thump; two small neck pops; Brayden's line with one censor beep. Under everything: a quiet basement room tone, the low hum of the water heater and the faint buzz of the bulb.

12 PRINCIPLES OF ANIMATION (GROUNDED VERSION FOR THIS FILM)

1. Squash and stretch: almost none. His face and body keep their shape; even the helmet sliding over his face is a rigid object moving, not a stretch.
2. Anticipation: small and natural. He plants his feet before he pulls, lifts the helmet high before he drops it, and stands still before the neck crack.
3. Staging: one clear action per shot: the pull, the glow, the manual, the paint, the helmet, the line.
4. Straight ahead and pose to pose: continuous, natural motion with no snapping into poses; each shot ends on a clean, held pose.
5. Follow-through: subtle. Dust settles after each tug, the lid settles on its hinges, the helmet wobbles once and settles.
6. Slow in and slow out: every move eases at human speed; the neck tilts are slow and deliberate.
7. Arcs: the lid swings up on a clean arc, his fingers draw the paint in smooth arcs, and the helmet comes down on a straight, heavy drop.
8. Secondary action: dust in the bulb light and the daylight shaft, and the faint hum of the basement, never pulling focus.
9. Timing: quick cuts, but real-speed acting inside each shot; the final hero shot is the slowest.
10. Exaggeration: minimal. The comedy is his total seriousness: an action-movie gear-up done by Brayden in a basement.
11. Solid drawing: consistent anatomy and volume in every frame; the footlocker, manual, tin and helmet stay solid; five fingers.
12. Appeal: a funny, charismatic schemer who believes he's an action hero. It's a comedy.
